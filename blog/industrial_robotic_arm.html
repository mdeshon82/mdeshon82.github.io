Reverse Engineering a Kawasaki Industrial Robot and Building My Own Controller

Matt Deshon • October 7, 2026

One of my more ambitious controls projects started with a used Kawasaki industrial robot arm, a pile of incomplete documentation, some controller backups, and one major problem: I did not have a practical way to run the robot.

The robot is a Kawasaki BA006L-A, a six-axis industrial arm originally designed for applications such as arc welding and automated material handling. Instead of trying to return the machine to its original factory configuration at any cost, I decided to approach it as a reverse engineering project.

The goal is now to preserve the original mechanical arm, motors, brakes, encoders, gearboxes, and internal wiring while replacing the original motion-control system with a modern controller built around ROS 2, EtherCAT, MATLAB, custom servo electronics, and a new control cabinet.

Starting with an Unknown Industrial Robot

Used industrial robots can be deceptively inexpensive. The robot itself may cost less than many hobby CNC machines, but the controller, teach pendant, proprietary cables, software, and replacement components can quickly become the expensive part of the project.

My BA006L came with enough information to identify the robot and original controller family, but bringing it back into service meant understanding several systems at the same time:

  • Six AC servo motors and their mechanical brakes
  • Battery-backed absolute encoders
  • Motor power wiring
  • Encoder and signal wiring
  • Robot mastering and zero-position data
  • Safety circuitry
  • Servo drive requirements
  • Motion-control software

The project quickly became less about simply “making the robot move” and more about reconstructing enough of the machine's original architecture to safely replace it.

Reverse Engineering the Robot Wiring

The first major task was determining what the large robot-side connectors actually carried. Kawasaki separates the arm wiring into two major harnesses. 

X3 / X3A
Encoder signals
Absolute position data
Battery and encoder power
Limit and auxiliary signals

X4 / X4A
Three-phase motor power
Mechanical brake circuits
Motor temperature signals
Auxiliary wiring
Protective earth

That distinction is important. X3 is largely low-level signal and encoder wiring, while X4 carries motor and brake power. Mixing assumptions between the two could damage expensive encoder electronics or motor hardware.

I began building pin maps from Kawasaki documentation, controller information, photographs, and continuity information. One example is the JT1 motor phase wiring: 

JT1 Motor

X4A-1   -> U
X4A-13  -> V
X4A-25  -> W

JT1 Brake

X4A-37
X4A-49

The encoder side was traced separately. JT1 uses the first encoder pair on X3A, with the other axes following the same general connector organization.

Important: Nothing gets energized simply because a pin number appears in a drawing. The final wiring will be continuity checked against the physical robot before power is ever applied. One of the recovered X4 references even appears to contain a JT5 labeling error, which is exactly why reverse engineering industrial hardware requires verification instead of assumption.

Identifying the Servo Motors

Motor identification is one of the most important parts of the retrofit because the servo amplifier, encoder interface, current limits, and control algorithms all depend on the motor characteristics.

JT1 and JT2 have now been positively identified as: 

Manufacturer: Sanyo Denki
Family:       SANMOTION R
Model:        R2AA13120LCP2F
Power:        1.2 kW
Rated Speed:  2000 RPM
Rated Torque: 5.7 Nm
Rated Current: 7.6 Arms
Kawasaki P/N: 50601-0144

These motors use battery-backed absolute encoders, which is one reason I want to preserve the factory motors instead of replacing the entire servo system.

Based on Kawasaki motor documentation, the remaining joints use progressively smaller motors, including approximately 500 watt, 100 watt, and sub-100 watt motors as the arm gets closer to the wrist. I am still verifying the exact suffixes and encoder variants for those motors before freezing the drive design.

Recovering the Original Robot Configuration

Building the Software Stack Before the Hardware

Rather than waiting until the new electronics exist, I built the motion-control software first. The current development environment uses ROS 2 Jazzy on Ubuntu 24.04 with a PREEMPT_RT kernel.

The robot model, joint limits, MoveIt configuration, ros2_control hardware layer, trajectory controller, status handling, and commissioning tools are already running in simulation.

MATLAB as the Engineering Workstation

MATLAB has become the engineering and commissioning side of the system. I have been using it for ROS communication, robot kinematics, drive calculations, motor modeling, and a custom workstation GUI.

The MATLAB workstation can already publish ROS 2 joint-jog commands to the Linux controller and receive feedback from the simulated robot.

The next major use for MATLAB has been determining whether the original 200 volt-class motors can be operated from a much lower DC bus at reduced speed.

Why I Decided Not to Buy Six Commercial Servo Drives

My first design used six commercial EtherCAT servo amplifiers. Technically, that would be the simplest solution. Financially, it changed the project completely.

By the time six compatible drives, safety hardware, power supplies, regeneration hardware, and cabinet components were added together, the servo-drive portion alone could easily reach several thousand dollars.

That led to a much more interesting engineering decision:

Design the servo controllers myself.

Instead of reproducing the original Kawasaki 200 VAC servo system, I am investigating a lower-voltage architecture designed around the way I actually intend to use the robot.

A 48 Volt Servo System

This robot is not going back onto a high-speed automotive production line. During development, I expect to operate it at a fraction of its original speed.

That changes the drive problem considerably.

The proposed architecture is now: 

208 VAC Single Phase
        |
Isolated 48 VDC Power Supply
        |
48 VDC Servo Bus
        |
+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+-------+
|       |       |       |       |       |
JT1     JT2     JT3     JT4     JT5     JT6
Drive   Drive   Drive   Drive   Drive   Drive
|       |       |       |       |       |
+--------------- Kawasaki BA006L ----------------+

Each axis will eventually have its own three-phase MOSFET inverter, current sensing, encoder interface, gate driver, brake control, fault monitoring, and real-time motor-control processor.

A central EtherCAT controller will connect those axes to ROS 2.

Testing the 48 Volt Idea in MATLAB

Before drawing a circuit board, I wanted to know whether a 48 volt DC bus was even reasonable for the large 1.2 kW JT1 and JT2 motors.

The initial MATLAB model used the known motor parameters: 

Rated Torque:              5.7 Nm
Rated Current:             7.6 Arms
Peak Current:              26.5 Arms
Torque Constant:           0.77 Nm/Arms
Phase Resistance:          0.35 ohm
Electrical Time Constant:  15 ms
Estimated Phase Inductance: 5.25 mH

I modeled 48, 60, and 72 volt DC buses while accounting for winding resistance, winding inductance, back EMF, electrical frequency, and SVPWM voltage limits.

The results were encouraging. 

48 VDC BUS

At 100 RPM:
Large voltage margin available

At 200 RPM:
Large voltage margin available

At 400 RPM:
Full rated torque remains practical

Around 500 RPM:
Voltage margin begins becoming dependent on motor pole count

For a robot I expect to initially operate around 5 to 10 percent of original rated speed, 48 volts appears to be a practical starting point.

Sizing the DIY Power Stage

The next MATLAB model looked at the actual inverter requirements.

The large JT1/JT2 motors require: 

Rated Phase Current:       7.6 Arms
Rated Sinusoidal Peak:     10.75 A

Published Peak Current:    26.5 Arms
Peak Sinusoidal Current:   37.48 Apeak

With engineering margin, the preliminary drive target is: 

DC Bus:                    48 V nominal

MOSFET Voltage Class:      100 V

Continuous Phase Current:  >= 12 Arms

Transient Phase Current:   ~55 to 60 A peak

Current Measurement:       +/- 60 A

DC Link Capacitors:        80 V minimum
                           100 V preferred

The 26.5 amp motor rating is a transient peak, not a continuous operating point. At that current, calculated copper loss is roughly 737 watts, which makes current limiting and thermal I²t protection mandatory in the final controller.

The Future Servo Drive

The current concept for each large-axis drive looks roughly like this: 

48 VDC BUS
    |
DC Link Capacitors
    |
Three-Phase MOSFET Bridge
    |
   U V W
    |
Sanyo Servo Motor


Real-Time MCU
    |
    +---- PWM / FOC
    |
    +---- Phase Current Sensors
    |
    +---- DC Bus Voltage
    |
    +---- Absolute Encoder Interface
    |
    +---- Brake Control
    |
    +---- Temperature / Fault Monitoring
    |
    +---- High-Speed Communications

Field-oriented control, or FOC, will be performed locally on the axis controller. ROS 2 will not generate MOSFET PWM directly. The Linux system will command motion, while the dedicated motor controller closes the high-speed current and velocity loops.

That separation is important. Linux and ROS are excellent for planning, kinematics, trajectory generation, diagnostics, and high-level control. The servo-current loop belongs on deterministic embedded hardware.

NI Multisim and Circuit Design

I have also added NI Circuit Design Suite 14.1 to the project environment. Multisim will be used to develop and test the electronics before committing to real hardware.

The first circuit will be a single 48 volt MOSFET half-bridge driving an electrical model of the JT1/JT2 motor winding: 

Motor Winding Model

Resistance:   0.35 ohm
Inductance:   5.25 mH

Once one half-bridge is behaving correctly, the simulation will expand to a complete three-phase inverter, current sensing, gate-driver circuitry, overcurrent protection, DC bus monitoring, and eventually a full motor-control stage.

Safety Still Stays Hardware-Based

One part of this project I am not moving into ROS software is the primary personnel safety system.

The intended safety architecture will use hardwired components for E-stop and drive disable functions. ROS will be able to monitor safety state, but software running on a general-purpose operating system will not be the only thing preventing motor torque. 

Emergency Stop
      |
Dual-Channel Safety Circuit
      |
      +---- Main Servo Power Contactor
      |
      +---- Hardware Drive Disable
                |
                +---- Axis Drives

Brake control will also follow a defined sequence. The mechanical brakes are holding brakes, not substitutes for controlled deceleration.

Final Thoughts

What started as a used industrial robot has turned into a project that touches nearly every part of modern controls engineering: robotics, embedded systems, industrial networking, servo control, power electronics, CAD, safety systems, real-time Linux, and software architecture.

The interesting part is not simply getting the BA006L to move again. Buying a complete replacement controller could accomplish that.

The real project is understanding the machine well enough to replace its original control system with one designed from the ground up, while preserving the precision mechanical hardware Kawasaki originally built into the arm.

There is still a lot of engineering between the current simulation and a powered robot. The encoder interface, inverter hardware, brake controls, PCB design, thermal management, regeneration, safety hardware, and physical commissioning all still need to be completed and tested.

That is also what makes the project worth doing.

The next update will move into the electronics side as I start building and simulating the first 48 volt servo inverter in NI Multisim.
